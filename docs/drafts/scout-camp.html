Scout Camp

Tuesday, 30 June 2026 2026
S

I spent last week with my daughter at Camp Ross, part of the Goshen Scouting Reservation. About three hours southwest of DC, Goshen is a large patch of Virginia centered on Lake Merriweather--named for Marjorie Merriweather Post, of the Post Foods dynasty, who bequeathed the property to the Scouts. The menu served at camp is a model of extreme frugality--a scout is thrifty, after all--with the notable exception of name-brand breakfast cereal.

There are several camps situated around the lake, and a backpacking program called Lenhok'sin that travels between them. Most of the camps serve scouts in 6th grade and up. Ross is the only one devoted to cub scouts in grades 4 and 5.

(There used to be two cub camps, but PMI was shut down years ago due to declining demand. Too bad, but having an abandoned summer camp just across the lake is a useful amenity when telling campfire ghost stories.)

I never went to Ross as a kid, but I attended Camp Bowman many times, which is best known for the disrepair of its shower facilities and the fact that scouts cook all their meals over open wood fires, rather than visiting a dining hall like at the other camps. Successive layers of bug spray, sunscreen, wood smoke, lake water, and spilled drink mix, combined with youth protection rules that prevent verifiable bathing, produce sedimentary levels of filth that are truly remarkable. To this day I feel a surge of stoic vigor whenever I smell wood smoke in the daylight.

I also did a trek at Lenhok'sin, though the only thing I really remember about it is getting accidentally shoved into tree by some roughhousing scouts and unluckily bracing my hand against a Goshen Zapper.

There was also one year when I got so deliriously sick that I was moved to the normally scout-less administrative camp, where I drifted in and out of fever, spending my waking hours on visits from my scoutmasters, a huge pile of yellowing Disney comics, worry over the fact that my exhalations smelled a lot like lake mud, and unfettered access to a freezer of push-pops similar to the one pictured above (for days, these were the only thing I ate). Alas, the lack of Flintstones branding and orange sherbet flavor kept the one pictured above from triggering a fully Proustian reverie. Still pretty good, though.

Camp is where the scout experience reaches apotheosis. Scouts are stuck in the woods, with other scouts, for at least a week. It will be uncomfortable in various ways, and their skills, ingenuity, and grit will determine the toll it takes on them. They will inevitably learn things about how the natural world works that are hard to intuit from a city or suburb: its beauty, its complexity, its hostility and bounty and indifference.

And they will gain access to experiences that kids can't get anywhere else. The scouts' safety and insurance underwriting protocols veered into a distinct evolutionary branch many years ago, sprouting off from the Red Cross-defined mainstream to which most youth organizations adhere. The scouts' procedures can feel baroque and onerous, but they also mean that kids can pilot a canoe, start a fire, shoot a gun, use a knife, or range over camp without adults, in ways that, for most comparable organizations, have been unimaginable for decades.

The hope is that this lets the kids learn about themselves and what they're capable of. In my experience, it does. My daughter started last week scandalized by the very idea of spiders. By Wednesday she was happily splashing with her friends in a swimming hole we'd hiked to, whose peripheral rocks housed a family of quite-prominent water snakes.

I admit my nerves were up. I'd been reading Lonesome Dove, and a night or two before had finished the chapter in which a hapless Irish immigrant is abruptly swarmed and killed by a nest of water moccasins during an otherwise peaceful river crossing.

(That still compares favorably to the frame of mind I was in during the week I spent at camp reading Crime and Punishment during a leadership program without running water, during which I was assigned--by my scoutmaster, who knew and trusted me--to a patrol of scouts from other troops with behavioral problems.)

Snakes aside, Lonesome Dove makes good camp reading. To imagine a version of outdoor life that is vastly poorer in materials--where a tent was a rare luxury, and canvas and leather were state-of-the-art--makes one feel much better about the greasy adhesion of dried sweat to a nylon sleeping bag. It leaves you confident, proud, and wary to understand what humans can achieve, ensure, or lose.

I believe deeply in these familiar lessons and was ecstatic at the chance to reexperience them, self-consciously reminding myself that the week was for the kids, not the adults. But this was also a chance to see scouting, and how it shaped me, through my newly middle-aged eyes, and perhaps even to understand some of the limits of the virtues it instills.

I believe, for example, that stoic endurance is admirable. It's good to be able to adapt to circumstances, to accept what can't be changed, and to make the best of what's before you. Taken too far, this is passivity, settling. There is a place for neurotic restlessness and ambition.

Watching the kids provided other revelations. And not just the cubs, though these had their quirks, too. The camp staff was almost entirely teenaged. Overhearing the gawky scoutcraft team complain bitterly about the sun-kissed and muscular aquatics staffers reminded me of an adolescent bestiary I'd started to believe only existed in teen dramas.

This is one of the best things about scouting, of course: it brings together all kinds of kids and forces them to cooperate, from golden-boy future astronauts to profoundly annoying autists.

(And now girls, too! The prospect of witnessing the implied interactions at the teenaged Troop level is terrifying--something completely absent from my own experience of scouting.)

The program has something to teach every kind of kid. But the astronauts are also spending their weekends hitting home runs and going on dates. For the weirdos, scouting is sometimes the best available way to feel possessed of some measure of fortitude. Unsurprisingly, a glance at the ranks of adult leaders shows that scouting attracts an outsize share of such weirdos. It was interesting to ponder my past and current place within their ranks.

But mostly it was wonderful to be able to watch my daughter grow in ways that I knew I once had, too. She is a brilliant girl, charming and goofy. After a week in the woods she seems tougher, less solipsistic. She was sunburned and happy. We're already talking about next year.
